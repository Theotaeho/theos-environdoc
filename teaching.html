Courses, Mentoring & Philosophy

Teaching

My teaching philosophy begins with the belief that education outlives the classroom. What students learn, and how they learn to approach knowledge, shapes the people, institutions and communities they will go on to influence.

01

Teaching Philosophy

I treat teaching as an ethical responsibility: to treat students with respect, to model scholarly integrity, and to teach accurate knowledge with care. My goal is to prepare students to become thoughtful leaders in academia and industry who contribute to building sustainable and thriving communities.

Creativity and independent problem solving emerge from a firm grasp of fundamentals, so I clarify the physical, mathematical or statistical meaning of a core concept and connect it to an authentic engineering example before moving to application. Students should come away understanding not only how to apply a method, but why it works, when it is appropriate, and how it extends to problems they have not seen.

Taken together, these commitments are meant to build perseverance and resilience, and the confidence to produce robust solutions under uncertainty.

02

Teaching Strategies

Building rapport

Learning names, greeting students outside class, and sharing lessons from my own academic experience — so that asking a question feels ordinary rather than costly.

Strong foundations first

The meaning of a concept before its application, connected to real environmental and engineering examples.

Clear objectives, checked often

Stated learning goals at the start of each class, then brief questions and informal comprehension checks to surface misconceptions early.

Genuine teamwork

Open-ended problems where students must explain their reasoning, weigh alternatives, and integrate individual contributions into one coherent solution.

03

Teaching Goals

Independent Earth-system thinkers

Students learn to see the environment as an interconnected system, to identify the mechanisms that matter most, and to recognize which questions remain unresolved.

Responsible collaborators

Open-ended projects that require exchanging ideas and integrating perspectives, so students learn that collaboration rests on reliability and intellectual honesty.

Readiness for an AI-enabled profession

Practical training in programming, statistics and AI, paired with the scientific judgment needed to evaluate what a computation actually tells you.

05

Mentoring

My mentoring spans a range of environmental topics and students from varied academic and cultural backgrounds. At the University of Michigan I have mentored two undergraduate and two graduate students on machine-learning approaches for predicting persistent environmental contaminants, and on integrated hydrological modeling combining physics-based simulation, surrogate modeling and uncertainty quantification. At the University of Brasília I advised students on sapflow analysis under inundation conditions in the Amazon. At the University of Seoul I mentored three graduate and ten undergraduate students through weekly meetings on global high-resolution satellite chlorophyll-a products, AI reconstruction of incomplete environmental time series, genetic-algorithm optimization of air-quality monitoring networks, and watershed-scale water quality modeling. These efforts produced three peer-reviewed publications.

06

Teaching in the Age of AI

The role of an educator now extends beyond transmitting established knowledge to cultivating informed judgment. I frame AI as a tool for extending human reasoning rather than replacing it. Through project-based learning grounded in authentic environmental and engineering problems, students integrate theory, data and computation while documenting how AI was used and defending the scientific validity of their conclusions. To keep access equitable, I provide structured foundational support alongside progressively more advanced activities — including introductory “Coding 101” and “Statistics 101” sessions — so that differences in prior preparation do not become barriers.
