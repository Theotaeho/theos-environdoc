Arctic: Arctic Greening and Energy Partitioning

Abstract: Accurate measurement of net radiation in the high-latitude Arctic regions is challenging since rain and snow events often introduce substantial measurement errors. To reduce the precipitation-induced measurement errors of downward radiation, customized data-driven methods are developed to reconstruct downward radiative fluxes from the biased radiation measurements. This study uses four years of field data across ten plots covered with forest, trees, and tundra in the Polar Urals from July 2018 to July 2022. Rain and snow on the radiometers absorb and block shortwave radiation and emit longwave radiation, leading to underestimation of downward shortwave and overestimation of downward longwave radiation. Snow causes more errors than rain. Seasonal variation of reconstructed net radiation for three dominant vegetation types indicates that their differences are most pronounced in April and least in September. Furthermore, forest and tree plots consistently exhibit higher magnitudes of net radiation and longer seasons of positive net radiation than tundra plots. This study advances methodologies for reconstructing corrupted net radiation data in the Arctic and offers insights into the variability of net radiation patterns within the forest-tundra ecotone.

Kim, T., Zhou, W., Tran, V. N., Zhang, L., Wang, J., Zhu, M., … & Ivanov, V. Y. (2025). Biases in radiative flux observations due to precipitation across the Arctic forest-tundra ecotone. Agricultural and Forest Meteorology, 374, 110814.

Amazon: Sapflow Functional Types

  • Heterogeneity in tree hydraulics and microclimate challenges the vital details of mechanistic understanding of tropical forest transpiration. We introduce an empirical framework that links sapflow diurnal dynamics to underlying biophysical mechanisms and defines distinct sapflow functional types (SFTs).
  • We monitored minute-scale sapflow in 50 trees (30 species) in an Amazonian rainforest over four years. From standardized clear-sky, non-dry conditions, we derived nine hydraulic behavior metrics from diurnal curves, tested predictability from tree traits, and identified SFTs with multivariate clustering.
  • Sapflow behavior was primarily predicted by tree structure and light environment, and secondarily by species functional traits. Seven distinct SFTs emerged, each contributing to ecosystem transpiration via unique diurnal dynamics. Using forest inventory data, we show that tree hydraulic heterogeneity reflects the distribution of traits and microenvironments. We provide a tool for approximating SFT distributions across tropical forests.
  • The SFT framework offers a scalable, mechanistic bridge between tree traits and forest transpiration, providing a foundation for simplifying ecohydrological analysis and improving representation of hydraulic diversity in tropical forest models.

Amazon: Sapflow ML/AI with Causal Interpretation

Abstract: Transpiration is a crucial flux transporting water from the subsurface to the atmosphere, thereby affecting both local and global water cycles. However, due to diversity of tropical forests, tree-level water use patterns driven by hydraulic trait variations remain poorly understood. Here, we investigate how tree sapflow varies seasonally and how the relative contributions of key environmental variables to sapflow change over time among trees in the Amazon rainforest. Sapflow data were collected from 34 trees at the KM67 site in the Amazon rainforest over a five-year period (2014–2018) using the thermal dissipation method. By integrating eddy covariance tower data, we selected eight predictor variables for sapflow modeling. For each tree, an artificial neural network (ANN) was trained to predict hourly sapflow, with hyperparameters optimized using Bayesian optimization based on the Tree-structured Parzen Estimator. The final ANN model of transpiration flux for each tree demonstrated high predictive performance for sapflow and was capable to quantify uncertainty in its predictions. After model training, we performed post-hoc interpretation based on causal Shapley values that describe hierarchical relationships among input variables. This framework provides both local and global interpretations of the temporally varying contributions of input variables. Furthermore, by addressing the challenges associated with highly correlated inputs and their causal relationships, this approach enables a more reliable assessment of each variable’s contribution. Our results reveal that sapflow patterns and the diurnal importance of key hydrometeorological variables varies among tree species and traits, as well as between dry and wet seasons in the Amazon rainforest. These differences become even more pronounced during the extreme drought of the 2015/2016 ENSO event. This study provides new insights into the mechanisms underlying tree water use strategies in tropical forests, highlighting the importance of dynamic interpretation for understanding sapflow responses to changing environmental conditions.

EcoLPT: Lagrangian Parcel Tracking Tool for Ecohydrology

Abstract: Lagrangian parcel tracking (LPT) reconstructs water-movement histories, revealing flow paths, travel distances, and residence times that are difficult to obtain from conventional Eulerian approaches. By following water parcels, LPT provides a process-based view of transport, storage, and fate. We present EcoLPT, an open-source LPT framework that operates as a post-processing tool for ecohydrological models. EcoLPT builds on legacy approaches such as EcoSLIM while adding novel methodological and computational advances through its implementation in OpenFOAM. Specifically, the framework supports integrated surface–subsurface simulations on unstructured polyhedral meshes, enabling applications in complex terrain and heterogeneous landscapes. EcoLPT characterizes flow domain memory and hydrologic connectivity through water parcel age, trajectories, path lengths, source attribution, domain transitions, and backward-tracking diagnostics. Its object-oriented architecture enables flexible customization and incorporation of additional transport processes, parcel attributes, and tracking strategies. We demonstrate EcoLPT through three applications: a 1D Richards-equation benchmark, a 2D hillslope experiment representing a simplified scenario of the coupled surface–subsurface interactions, and a 3D watershed-scale application using an unstructured mesh with real-world topographic and hydroclimatic conditions. In all applications, EcoLPT uses state and flux outputs from the Advanced Terrestrial Simulator (ATS), showing how parcel-based water-history diagnostics complement ATS simulations. Across these examples, EcoLPT reveals dimensions of water history and transport behavior hidden in Eulerian state and flux fields. By complementing conventional ecohydrological simulations with interpretable parcel-based diagnostics, EcoLPT helps identify unresolved hydrologic patterns, improve understanding of process interactions, and support water-resources analysis and decision-making.

Kim, T., Le, P., Coon, E., Painter, S., Maxwell, R., Tran, V., Taylor, T., & Ivanov, V. EcoLPT v1.0: Lagrangian Particle Tracking tool for Ecohydrology. Geoscientific Model Development (In preparation).

ExET: Extreme Evapotranspiration

Summary: Terrestrial ecosystems transfer water from soils and vegetation to the atmosphere through evapotranspiration (ET), linking the water, energy, and carbon cycles. During hydroclimatic extremes, high atmospheric demand, heat, and limited precipitation can trigger rapid surges in ecosystem water loss, potentially accelerating flash drought and persistent drought. Yet it remains unclear when these events support productive water use and when they signal emerging stress. This project defines extreme evapotranspiration (ExET) as an observation-based indicator of rapid water-loss shocks and asks: What drives ExET, how do ecosystems respond and recover, and where will vulnerability increase under future change? We hypothesize that ExET contributes disproportionately to total ET, with impacts varying across hydroclimatic and vegetation gradients. ExET may initially support carbon uptake, but prolonged events are expected to deplete soil moisture, constrain stomatal conductance, and reduce water-use efficiency.

The project will integrate NASA Earth observations with global flux-tower measurements through four objectives. O1: ExET events will be identified using seasonally varying ET thresholds, with consecutive exceedances grouped into events and quantified by frequency, duration, intensity, and cumulative severity. Ecosystem water-loss stability will be evaluated using temporal stability, resistance, recovery, and resilience to determine how vegetation influences buffering and post-event recovery. O2: Hydroclimatic and vegetation properties will be analyzed to identify the dominant controls on ExET. Predictive models will evaluate how meteorological forcing, soil-water availability, vegetation structure, and stomatal regulation govern the fraction of total ET associated with ExET across sites, biomes, and temporal scales. O3: Event-centered trajectories will quantify cascading responses in soil moisture, conductance, evapotranspiration partitioning, productivity, and water-use efficiency. These analyses will determine whether ExET reflects sustained productive water use or a transition toward water limitation, reduced carbon uptake, and persistent ecosystem stress, and how these responses vary across vegetation types. O4: Observation-constrained relationships will be scaled using NASA Earth observations, including soil moisture, vegetation, energy-balance, and ET products, to generate monthly and annual maps of ExET contribution and associated uncertainty. NASA climate projections will assess changes through 2100 and separate hydroclimatic effects from shifts in vegetation buffering capacity.

Integrated Ecohydrological Model

The observational and methodological work meets here. I develop integrated ecohydrological models — the Advanced Terrestrial Simulator and tRIBS+VEGGIE — for Amazon watersheds, resolving coupled surface, subsurface and vegetation processes against the field record collected at the same sites.

Because these simulations are expensive, I also build AI surrogate models that reproduce their behaviour at a fraction of the cost, making ensemble experiments and uncertainty quantification tractable.
